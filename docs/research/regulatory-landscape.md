## 1. EU MDR/IVDR software classification landscape

### 1.1 MDR Article 2(1) — definition of "medical device"

Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR), Article 2(1), defines:

> "'medical device' means any instrument, apparatus, appliance, software, implant, reagent, material or other article intended by the manufacturer to be used, alone or in combination, for human beings for one or more of the following specific medical purposes:
> — diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease,
> — diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury or disability,
> — investigation, replacement or modification of the anatomy or of a physiological or pathological process or state,
> — providing information by means of *in vitro* examination of specimens derived from the human body, including organ, blood and tissue donations,
> and which does not achieve its principal intended action by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means, in or on the human body, but which may be assisted in its function by such means."

Software is explicitly named in the definition, and MDR Article 2(4) separately provides that "software shall also be deemed to be an active device." This text was retrieved both from the UK's official statute-law database's reproduction of the retained/adopted EU regulation text ([legislation.gov.uk, Article 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/745/article/2/adopted)) and independently confirmed verbatim (with footnote to "Article 2(1) of Regulation (EU) 2017/745 – MDR") inside the official MDCG 2019-11 guidance document (see 1.2 below), so it is corroborated by two independent renderings of the primary EU text. Whether software that provides diagnostic/therapeutic decision support falls within this definition turns on whether it is intended for one of the listed "specific medical purposes" — MDCG 2019-11 (below) is the operative EU-level guidance on how to apply this test to software specifically.

### 1.2 MDCG 2019-11 — Guidance on Qualification and Classification of Software in MDR and IVDR

Full primary source fetched directly: [MDCG 2019-11, October 2019](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_en_0.pdf) (Medical Device Coordination Group, published on the European Commission's health/medical-devices site). The document states its own status: "This document has been endorsed by the [MDCG]... The document is not a European Commission document and it cannot be regarded as reflecting the official position of the European Commission. Any views expressed in this document are not legally binding and only the Court of Justice of the European Union can give binding interpretations of Union law." It is nonetheless the operative EU-level interpretive guidance used in practice for software qualification/classification.

**Qualification (is it a medical device at all).** MDCG 2019-11 §3.1 states the threshold principle: "Software must have a medical purpose on its own to be qualified as a medical device software (MDSW)... It must be highlighted that the risk of harm to patients, users of the software, or any other person, related to the use of the software within healthcare, including a possible malfunction is **not** a criterion on whether the software qualifies as a medical device" [emphasis added — i.e. qualification and classification are separate, sequential questions].

The guidance sets out a five-step decision flowchart (Figure 1, §3.3) to determine whether a piece of software is Medical Device Software (MDSW) at all:
- Step 1: Is the product "software" per the guidance's definition (a set of instructions that processes input data and creates output data)?
- Step 2: Is it an MDR Annex XVI device, an accessory to a medical device, or software driving/influencing the use of a (hardware) medical device? (If so, it is regulated as part of/accessory to that device, not independently.)
- Step 3: Does it perform an action on data beyond storage, archival, communication, or simple search?
- Step 4: Is the action for the benefit of individual patients (as opposed to e.g. only aggregating population data, generic/non-patient-specific pathways, or epidemiological registers)?
- Step 5: Does it meet the MDSW definition (i.e., does it fulfil Article 2(1) MDR / Article 2(2) IVDR)?

The guidance's own worked example category **"Decision Support Software"** (§9(b)) states: "In general, these are computer based tools which combine general medical information databases and algorithms with patient-specific data. They are intended to provide healthcare professionals and/or users with recommendations for diagnosis, prognosis, monitoring and treatment of individual patients. Based on Figure 1, they are qualified as medical devices." It gives worked examples including radiotherapy treatment planning systems, chemotherapy drug-dosage planning systems, and Computer Aided Detection systems ("intended to provide information that may suggest or exclude medical conditions... For example, such systems would be able to automatically analyse x-ray images or interpret ECGs").

**On decision-support vs. autonomous decision-making.** MDCG 2019-11 does *not* draw a bright-line qualification exemption for software that "leaves the decision to a healthcare professional" as opposed to acting autonomously — leaving the decision to a clinician is treated as the normal mode of operation of MDSW generally, not as a basis for falling outside the device definition. §3.1 states plainly: "Software can directly control a (hardware) medical device (e.g. radiotherapy treatment software), can provide immediate decision-triggering information (e.g. blood glucose meter software), **or can provide support for healthcare professionals** (e.g. ECG interpretation software)" — all three modes are treated as potentially MDSW; the "support for healthcare professionals" framing does not itself exempt the software from qualification. Where the decision-support/autonomy distinction *does* appear in the guidance is at the **classification** stage (§4.2.1, Rule 11 — see 1.3), where the guidance repeatedly frames risk classification in terms of "the significance of the information provided by the software to the healthcare decision" (citing IMDRF/SaMD WG/N12FINAL:2014) — i.e., how much weight a clinician's decision places on the software's output — combined with the severity of the underlying "healthcare situation or patient condition." This is a risk-classification factor, not a qualification (in/out of scope) test.

### 1.3 MDR Annex VIII, Chapter III, Rule 11 — software classification rule (exact text)

Retrieved from the UK's official reproduction of the adopted MDR Annex VIII ([legislation.gov.uk, Annex VIII](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2017/745/annex/VIII/adopted)) and cross-verified verbatim against the quotation of "Rule 11" reproduced in MDCG 2019-11 §4.2.1. Full text of Rule 11:

> "Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes is classified as class IIa, except if such decisions have an impact that may cause:
> — death or an irreversible deterioration of a person's state of health, in which case it is in class III; or
> — a serious deterioration of a person's state of health or a surgical intervention, in which case it is classified as class IIb.
> Software intended to monitor physiological processes is classified as class IIa, except if it is intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters, where the nature of variations of those parameters is such that it could result in immediate danger to the patient, in which case it is classified as class IIb.
> All other software is classified as class I."

MDCG 2019-11 §4.2.1 breaks this into three sub-rules and clarifies their scope and relationship:

- **Sub-rule 11a** (first three paragraphs — diagnostic/therapeutic decision information): "this sub-rule is generally applicable to all MDSW (excluding those MDSW that have no medical purpose)." Default is Class IIa; escalates to Class III where an incorrect decision informed by the software could reasonably cause "death or an irreversible deterioration of a person's state of health," or to Class IIb where it could cause "serious deterioration of a person's state of health or surgical intervention." The guidance notes these two exceptions "are mainly intended to apply a risk classification based on the significance of the provided information and the potential impact of an (incorrect) decision made using information from the MDSW."
- **Sub-rule 11b** (physiological-process monitoring): a specific rule for MDSW intended *only* for monitoring; Class IIa by default, Class IIb "if it is intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters" where variation could mean immediate danger. MDCG 2019-11 notes vital physiological parameters "include, for example, respiration, heart rate, cerebral functions, blood gases, blood pressure and body temperature."
- **Sub-rule 11c**: "all other MDSW is classified as class I."

**Related Annex VIII Implementing Rules, relevant to combination products** (also retrieved from legislation.gov.uk and quoted identically in MDCG 2019-11 §4.1):
- Implementing Rule 3.3: "Software, which drives or influences the use of a device, shall fall within the same class as the device. If the software is independent of any other device, it shall be classified in its own right." MDCG 2019-11 adds that where software both achieves its own intended purpose *and* drives/influences a hardware device, "the risk class shall not be lower than the risk class of the hardware medical device."
- Implementing Rule 3.5: "If several rules, or if, within the same rule, several sub-rules, apply to the same device based on the device's intended purpose, the strictest rule and sub-rule resulting in the higher classification shall apply."

**MDCG 2019-11 Annex III — IMDRF risk-categorisation cross-reference table** (explicitly stated to be for "illustrative purposes only" and not itself normative) maps a 3×3 grid of (i) "significance of information provided by the MDSW to a healthcare situation" (High="treat or diagnose" / Medium="drives clinical management" / Low="informs clinical management") against (ii) "state of healthcare situation or patient condition" (Critical / Serious / Non-serious) onto the Rule 11 classes. Per that table: Critical+High → Class III; Critical+Medium and Serious+High → Class IIb; all other combinations shown → Class IIa (the table explicitly excludes Class I, i.e. it only orients within Rule 11a's non-Class-I space).

MDCG 2019-11 Annex IV gives worked classification examples directly on point for guideline-based clinical software, without drawing any conclusion for this specific application:
- "MDSW intended to rank therapeutic suggestions for a health care professional based on patient history, imaging test results, and patient characteristics, for example, MDSW that lists and ranks all available chemotherapy options for BRCA-positive individuals, should be classified as class IIa per Rule 11(a) — IMDRF Risk Category II.i as it informs clinical management for cancer, a critical disease."
- "Cognitive therapy MDSW that includes a diagnostic function which is intended to feed back to the software to determine follow-up therapy... should be in class III per Rule 22. When a specialist determines the necessary cognitive therapy based on the outcome provided by the MDSW, the MDSW would be classified as class IIa per Rule 11(a)."
- "Diagnostic MDSW intended for scoring depression based on inputted data on a patient's symptoms (e.g. mood, anxiety) should be classified as class IIb under Rule 11(a) ... as the healthcare situation (depression) is serious and the significance of the information is 'diagnosis'."
- "MDSW app intended to support conception by calculating the user's fertility status based on a validated statistical algorithm [with a simple three-light output]... should be classified as class I per Rule 11c."

No conclusion is drawn here as to which class this application's Recommendation Sources would fall under; the primary-source criteria above are reported so a human reviewer can apply them.

### 1.4 IVDR relevance

IVDR (Regulation (EU) 2017/746), Article 2(2), defines "in vitro diagnostic medical device" (quoted verbatim inside MDCG 2019-11 §2, footnoted to "Article 2(2) of Regulation (EU) 2017/746 – IVDR"):

> "'In vitro diagnostic medical device' means any medical device which is a reagent, reagent product, calibrator, control material, kit, instrument, apparatus, piece of equipment, software or system, whether used alone or in combination, intended by the manufacturer to be used *in vitro* for the examination of specimens, including blood and tissue donations, derived from the human body, solely or principally for the purpose of providing information on one or more of the following: concerning a physiological or pathological process or state; concerning congenital physical or mental impairments; concerning the predisposition to a medical condition or a disease; to determine the safety and compatibility with potential recipients; to predict treatment response or reactions; to define or monitoring therapeutic measures. Specimen receptacles shall also be deemed to be *in vitro* diagnostic medical devices."

MDCG 2019-11 §3.4's decision steps (Figure 2) for allocating software between MDR and IVDR turn on whether the software's output/intended purpose is driven by data obtained from *in vitro* diagnostic devices (IVDR) versus other medical devices/clinical data such as imaging or clinician-entered findings (MDR): "If the data analysed is obtained from a combination of both *in vitro* diagnostic medical devices and medical devices... [decision step 3 asks] Is the intended purpose substantially driven by data sources coming from *in vitro* diagnostic medical devices?"

On the facts described for this project — a rule engine evaluating clinician-entered nodule findings (size, morphology, growth, patient risk factors) and imaging-derived characteristics against guideline rules, with no ingestion of laboratory/*in vitro* assay results — the software does not on its face process data "obtained by means of *in vitro* examination of specimens," which is the operative criterion in both the Article 2(2) IVDR definition and MDCG 2019-11's Decision Step 2/3 test. This is reported as a textual observation about why IVDR's stated scope does not appear to be engaged by the facts as described, not as a formal qualification determination.

## 2. GDPR considerations for stateless client-side processing

### 2.1 Article 4 definitions (verbatim)

Retrieved from the UK's official reproduction of the adopted GDPR text ([legislation.gov.uk, Article 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/4/adopted)) and cross-checked against a full-text transcription of the Regulation ([gdpr-info.eu, Art. 4](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/) — noted here as a secondary aggregator of the Official Journal text, used because it rendered the full itemised list in Art. 4(2) that legislation.gov.uk's summary omitted):

- **Article 4(1) "personal data":** "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('data subject')..."
- **Article 4(2) "processing":** "any operation or set of operations which is performed on personal data or on sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure or destruction."
- **Article 4(7) "controller":** "the natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data..."
- **Article 4(8) "processor":** "a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which processes personal data on behalf of the controller."

### 2.2 Article 9 — special categories of personal data (health data)

Retrieved from [legislation.gov.uk, Article 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/9/adopted):

- **Art. 9(1) (general prohibition):** "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, **data concerning health** or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be prohibited."
- **Art. 9(2)** lists exceptions, including (a) explicit consent; (h) processing "necessary for the purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, for the assessment of the working capacity of the employee, medical diagnosis, the provision of health or social care or treatment..." carried out by/under the responsibility of a professional subject to an obligation of professional secrecy; (i) substantial public interest in public health; (j) archiving/scientific/statistical purposes.

Whether "data concerning health" under Art. 9 is engaged turns on whether the transient, in-browser Case State data (clinician-entered nodule findings) constitutes "personal data" at all in the first place under Art. 4(1) — which in turn depends on whether it "relat[es] to an identified or identifiable natural person" as processed by this application. This document does not resolve that threshold question; it is flagged as the necessary first step before Art. 9's prohibition/exceptions become relevant.

### 2.3 Does "processing" under Art. 4(2) cover purely local, transient, client-side computation?

Article 4(2)'s definition is deliberately broad ("any operation ... whether or not by automated means") and is not textually limited to server-side or networked processing; local computation performed entirely on a device (e.g. "use," "adaptation or alteration," "combination") appears to fall within the ordinary meaning of the listed operations regardless of where the computation physically occurs. **No EDPB or WP29 opinion was found that speaks directly to the specific fact pattern of purely client-side, in-browser, never-transmitted, never-persisted computation of health-adjacent data.** This is a genuine primary-source gap: searches for EDPB/WP29 guidance specifically addressing "local-only" or "on-device" processing with zero server component did not surface an on-point opinion.

The closest analogous primary-source material found is **WP29 Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of "controller" and "processor" (WP169, adopted 16 February 2010)**, fetched in full from the European Commission's archived Article 29 Working Party documentation ([ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2010/wp169_en.pdf](https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2010/wp169_en.pdf)). This opinion predates the GDPR (it interprets Directive 95/46/EC's materially identical Article 2(b) processing definition and Article 2(d)/(e) controller/processor definitions) but its analysis is treated by EDPB and DPAs as the doctrinal foundation later carried into the GDPR-era Guidelines 07/2020 (see 2.4 below — full text of that later document could not be retrieved; see gap note).

Two passages from WP169 bear on the "who is responsible when there is no data transmission" question, though neither addresses the client-side-only scenario directly:

- On what makes an entity a "processor": WP169 §III.2 states two conditions must both be met — "being a separate legal entity with respect to the controller and ... processing personal data on his behalf" — i.e. the concept of "processor" under Art. 4(8)/old Art. 2(e) presupposes that the entity in fact *processes* the data. An entity that never receives or has any technical means to act on the data cannot, on this analysis, be a "processor," because there is no processing activity by that entity to attribute.
- On what makes an entity a "controller" (§III.1.b, discussing the "purposes and means" element): "According to a previous text presented by the Commission during the process of adoption of the Directive, having access to certain personal data would have entailed being (joint) controller for these data. However, this formulation was not retained in the final text and the experience shows that on the one hand **access to data does not entail as such control, while on the other hand having access to data is not an essential condition to be a controller**." WP169's broader analysis (§III.1.a) frames "controller" status as turning on the *factual* question of who "determines" the purposes and means of a processing operation — "why is this processing taking place? Who initiated it?" — rather than on physical possession of, or access to, the data itself.

Taken together, this is the closest primary-source material bearing on the question of whether a software provider whose application performs computation only in the end user's own browser, with the provider never receiving the data, could be characterised as a "processor" (WP169's own two-part test suggests no, absent any processing activity by the provider) or a "controller" (an open, fact-dependent question under WP169's "determines purposes and means" test, since — per WP169 — data access/receipt is expressly stated not to be an essential condition either way). No EDPB or WP29 material was found that applies this reasoning to the specific case of client-side software distribution.

### 2.4 Gap: EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of controller and processor

This is the GDPR-era successor to WP169, and the natural place to look for an updated, GDPR-specific version of the above analysis. The full guideline text could not be retrieved during this research: the EDPB's own PDF hosting URL returned an HTTP 404 at the time of this research, and no alternate authoritative mirror could be located and fetched. This is flagged explicitly as a **gap** — this document does not report the contents of EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 (2020, finalised) beyond what is described in secondary commentary (e.g. a Society for Computers & Law summary and a DigitalEurope consultation-response document, both surfaced in search results but not independently verified against the primary text), which describes a further-developed distinction between "essential means" (reserved to the controller) and "non-essential" technical/organisational means (delegable), and references an example concerning encrypted data where "the receiving person does not have any access nor has any available means to access personal data" and would not be considered a processor — consistent with, and apparently elaborating on, WP169's point above. This secondary characterisation should be verified against the primary EDPB text before being relied upon.

### 2.5 Household exemption and other GDPR hooks

Article 2(2)(c) GDPR excludes from the Regulation's scope processing "by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity." As the project context notes, this exemption is plainly inapplicable here regardless of the client-side architecture, because the processing (by a clinician, in a professional clinical context) is not personal/household activity. No EDPB or WP29 material was identified that carves out a parallel exemption, or even discusses a parallel scenario, for professional-context software that runs only client-side. This document does not identify any provision or guidance stating that GDPR's substantive obligations are switched off, or that no controller/processor relationship is formed, merely because an entity's software architecture never transmits data off-device — that determination is left, per WP169, to a fact-specific "determines the purposes and means" analysis for which no directly on-point primary guidance was located.

## 3. Clinical accountability & release authority in comparable CDS governance

### 3.1 IEC 62304 (Medical device software — Software life cycle processes)

The full text of IEC 62304 is copyright-protected and paywalled by IEC/ISO; it was **not** fully accessible during this research. A substantial free preview (covering the Foreword, Introduction, Scope (Clause 1), Normative References (Clause 2), and Terms and Definitions (Clause 3), i.e. roughly the first quarter of the standard) was retrieved via an iTeh-hosted standards preview ([IEC 62304:2006 preview, standards.iteh.ai](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/11630/3af7c2dc38c8489781331a49a001b0ff/IEC-62304-2006.pdf)); this preview does **not** include the operative Clause 4.3 ("Software safety classification") or Clause 5.8 ("Software release") text, which is exactly the material most relevant to this research question and which remains behind IEC's paywall. The description below of Class A/B/C is therefore sourced from secondary summaries (Johner Institute, Greenlight Guru) rather than the primary standard text, and is flagged as such.

From the primary preview text actually retrieved:

- **Scope/Purpose (Clause 1.1):** "This standard defines the life cycle requirements for MEDICAL DEVICE SOFTWARE. The set of PROCESSES, ACTIVITIES, and TASKS described in this standard establishes a common framework for MEDICAL DEVICE SOFTWARE life cycle PROCESSES."
- **Field of application (Clause 1.2):** "This standard applies to the development and maintenance of MEDICAL DEVICE SOFTWARE when software is itself a MEDICAL DEVICE or when software is an embedded or integral part of the final MEDICAL DEVICE. This standard does not cover validation and final release of the MEDICAL DEVICE, even when the MEDICAL DEVICE consists entirely of software."
- **Compliance (Clause 1.4):** "Compliance with this standard is defined as implementing all of the PROCESSES, ACTIVITIES, and TASKS identified in this standard in accordance with the software safety class... Compliance is determined by inspection of all documentation required by this standard including the RISK MANAGEMENT FILE, and assessment of the PROCESSES, ACTIVITIES and TASKS required for the software safety class."
- The standard's table of contents confirms the existence of "4.3 * Software safety classification" and "5.8 * Software release" as distinct normative clauses, and Annex A contains "Table A.1 – Summary of requirements by software safety class" — confirming that safety classification and a formal release step are both structurally central to the standard, even though this research could not retrieve their operative text.
- Relevant primary definitions retrieved from Clause 3: "RISK — combination of the probability of occurrence of HARM and the severity of that HARM" (citing ISO/IEC Guide 51:1999); "SAFETY — freedom from unacceptable RISK"; "SERIOUS INJURY — injury or illness that ... is life threatening, results in permanent impairment ... or necessitates medical or surgical intervention to prevent permanent impairment..."; "MANUFACTURER — natural or legal person with responsibility for designing, manufacturing, packaging, or labelling a MEDICAL DEVICE..."

Per secondary summaries (not independently verified against the primary Clause 4.3 text): IEC 62304 defines three software safety classes — Class A ("no injury or damage to health is possible"), Class B ("injury is possible, but not serious"), and Class C ("death or serious injury is possible") — with documentation, verification, and lifecycle-process rigor scaling up with class. IEC 62304 is described (secondary sources; not verified against IEC's own catalogue abstract, which also returned an access error during this research) as internationally recognised by FDA and other regulators as the harmonised standard for medical device software lifecycle processes. IEC 62304 does not itself name a required "who" for sign-off (e.g. it does not use a term equivalent to this project's "Approval Status" signatory) in the material actually retrieved; it structures compliance around the *manufacturer's* quality management system (Clause 4.1, cross-referencing ISO 13485) rather than prescribing a specific accountable individual/role, though this could not be fully confirmed against the (paywalled) Clause 5.8 "Software release" text.

### 3.2 NHS DCB0129 / DCB0160 (Clinical Risk Management standards, England)

**DCB0129** ("Clinical Risk Management: its Application in the Manufacture of Health IT Systems") applies to manufacturers/developers of health IT systems; **DCB0160** ("... in the Deployment and Use of Health IT Systems") applies to the adopting care organisation. Both are official NHS England Digital information standards. Primary-source confirmation of scope and legal status was obtained from an official NHS "AI and Digital Regulations Service" developer-guidance document, fetched and read directly ([digitalregulations.innovation.nhs.uk, "Complying with NHS Digital clinical risk management standards"](https://digitalregulations.innovation.nhs.uk/regulations-and-guidance-for-developers/all-developers-guidance/complying-with-nhs-digital-clinical-risk-management-standards/pdf/), downloaded 2026-08-29), which states in terms: "This is **required** guidance ... It is legally required and it is an essential activity," and: "If your digital technology cannot meet standard DCB0129, you will not be able to place it on the market. Adopters will not be able to use your technology in the NHS." The same document states DCB0129 requires developers to "detail and evidence that a clinical risk management system is in place," including "clinical risk management governance arrangements," "clinical risk management activities," and "clinical safety competence and training" — starting "at the earliest stage of your development lifecycle" and continuing through "maintenance and decommissioning."

The [NHS Standards Directory page for DCB0160](https://standards.nhs.uk/published-standards/clinical-risk-management-its-application-in-the-deployment-and-use-of-health-it-systems) confirms the standard's stated purpose: it "provides a set of requirements suitably structured to promote and ensure the effective application of clinical risk management by health and care organisations deploying and using Health IT Systems."

**Clinical Safety Officer (CSO).** Neither of the two official pages fetched above spells out, in the text actually retrieved, the CSO's required qualifications in detail. The specific requirement that the CSO be a currently-registered clinician (commonly cited as requiring registration with a body such as the GMC, NMC, GPhC, or HCPC, plus completion of NHS England's clinical safety training pathway) is reported here from **secondary sources only** (compliance-consultancy sites: Naq Cyber, Assuric, ProtectClinical) that were not independently cross-checked against the primary DCB0129/DCB0160 standard PDF text — attempts to fetch the standard PDFs directly either 404'd or returned binary content that could not be reliably parsed for this specific provision. This is flagged as a **gap**: the existence and centrality of a named "Clinical Safety Officer" role is corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources and by the official NHS guidance's reference to "clinical safety competence and training" as a required element of the developer's clinical risk management system, but the exact qualification/registration requirement wording has not been verified here against DCB0129/DCB0160's own primary text.

This is nonetheless the most directly on-point primary-source-adjacent material found for the underlying issue-#6 question ("who must the Approval Status signatory be"): DCB0129/DCB0160 are real, currently-in-force (as of this research; a public consultation on revising both standards was noted as open 29 June–11 September 2026) UK regulatory standards that name a specific accountable clinical role (the Clinical Safety Officer) as the person responsible for a manufacturer's clinical risk management activity for health IT software, distinct from the software's technical author(s).

### 3.3 FDA — Clinical Decision Support Software / Section 520(o) of the FD&C Act

The relevant primary legal text is Section 520(o)(1)(E) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (as added by the 21st Century Cures Act of 2016; codified at 21 U.S.C. § 360j(o)(1)(E)). The statutory language — retrieved from the U.S. Government Publishing Office's govinfo.gov text of the enacting bill ([govinfo.gov, BILLS-114s1101rs](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-114s1101rs/html/BILLS-114s1101rs.htm)) — excludes a software function from the statutory definition of "device" if it is not intended to acquire/process/analyze a medical image, a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device, or a pattern/signal from a signal acquisition system, **and** it meets all of the following:

> (i) "displaying, analyzing, or printing medical information about a patient or other medical information (such as peer-reviewed clinical studies and clinical practice guidelines)";
> (ii) "supporting or providing recommendations to a health care professional about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or condition"; and
> (iii) "enabling such health care professional to independently review the basis for such recommendations that such software presents so that it is not the intent that such health care professional rely primarily on any of such recommendations to make a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision regarding an individual patient."

FDA's implementing guidance, "Clinical Decision Support Software" (final guidance issued September 28, 2022, per the Federal Register notice at [federalregister.gov/documents/2022/09/28/2022-20993](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/09/28/2022-20993/clinical-decision-support-software-guidance-for-industry-and-food-and-drug-administration-staff), and subsequently revised — a further revision was reported in January 2026), interprets these criteria to distinguish "Non-Device CDS" (excluded from FDA device regulation because a clinician can independently evaluate the basis for the recommendation and is not intended to rely on it primarily) from CDS that remains a regulated device. **Note on source access:** this research located and cites the underlying statutory text directly from a primary U.S. government legislative source (govinfo.gov), but could not successfully fetch the FDA guidance document PDF itself (fda.gov links returned HTTP 404 at the URLs tried during this research), so the guidance's own worked examples and further interpretive detail beyond the bare statutory criteria are not reported here first-hand; this is flagged as a **gap**. Secondary legal-alert sources (Ropes & Gray, Arnold & Porter, Crowell & Moring) describing the guidance's practical application were surfaced in search but not relied upon for any specific quoted claim in this section.

The statutory criterion in clause (iii) is the most directly on-point primary-source language located anywhere in this research for the general regulatory concept this project's own framing ("leaving the clinical decision to the clinician," "no black box," full rationale/provenance surfaced per Recommendation") tracks descriptively — FDA's own statute distinguishes exactly along the axis of whether a clinician can independently review the basis for a recommendation rather than relying on it primarily. No conclusion is drawn as to whether this application's specific behavior would in fact satisfy FDA's "Non-Device CDS" criteria; that determination depends on facts (e.g., exact UI/UX treatment of provenance and rationale, marketing claims, etc.) beyond the scope of this document.

### 3.4 Other frameworks considered

NICE and WHO CDS-governance material, and a possible "clinical safety officer"-equivalent role in other jurisdictions, were not separately researched in comparable depth given time constraints; the DCB0129/0160 (England) and FDA Section 520(o) (US) material above represent the two strongest, most directly on-point primary/near-primary sources located for "who is accountable for approving a change to a clinical decision-support rule/algorithm."
